Wet Waste

What is wet waste —
and what goes in it?

August 11, 2026

Practical guides to a lower-waste home. Links marked amazon.in and amazon.com are affiliate links.

If you've ever stood at the kitchen bin wondering whether a tea bag or an eggshell counts as wet waste — you're not alone. Wet waste is one of the most talked-about waste categories and also one of the most misunderstood.

The good news is that once you understand what it is and what belongs in it, sorting becomes second nature. And wet waste, more than any other category, has the most useful second life — it can become rich compost or even cooking gas for your home.

"Nearly 50–60% of household waste is wet waste — and almost all of it can be turned into something useful instead of going to a landfill."

What is wet waste?

Wet waste refers to any organic, biodegradable material that was once living — food scraps, plant matter, and anything that will naturally decompose over time. It's called "wet" waste simply because it tends to have high moisture content compared to dry recyclables like paper or plastic.

Wet waste is also called organic waste or biodegradable waste. In India's municipal waste system, it goes into the green bin — the one meant for material that can be composted or processed into biogas.

The key characteristic of wet waste is that microorganisms can break it down naturally. When managed well, this process gives us compost — a nutrient-rich soil amendment that feeds plants and reduces the need for chemical fertilisers. When managed poorly — mixed with other waste in a landfill — it produces methane, a greenhouse gas far more potent than CO₂.

The green bin rule

If it was once alive and will rot on its own — it's wet waste. If it won't decompose naturally within a few months — it's not wet waste, even if it came from food.

Types of wet waste at home

Wet waste comes from several different parts of the home. Here's a breakdown of each type with examples.

🥦

Kitchen scraps — vegetable & fruit waste

The largest source of wet waste in any home. Every time you cook, you generate peels, seeds, cores, tops, and trimmings that are perfectly compostable.

This includes both raw and cooked produce waste — though raw scraps decompose faster and are better for home composting.

Vegetable peels Fruit skins & cores Onion skins Garlic peels Seed pods Corn husks Wilted greens Overripe fruit
🍚

Cooked food & leftovers

Leftover rice, curry, bread, roti, dal, cooked vegetables — all of these are wet waste. They decompose well in a compost pit or biogas unit, though they attract pests more than raw scraps do.

For home composting, avoid adding large amounts of oily or heavily spiced cooked food as they slow decomposition and attract rodents. Small amounts mixed with dry leaves work fine. For biogas units, cooked food is actually ideal — it breaks down quickly and produces good gas yield.

Leftover rice & roti Dal & curry Stale bread Cooked vegetables Noodles & pasta Spoiled food
☕

Tea & coffee waste

Tea leaves, used tea bags, and coffee grounds are some of the best materials you can add to a compost bin. They're rich in nitrogen, which helps speed up decomposition and feeds the microorganisms that break everything down.

Coffee grounds also improve soil aeration and are loved by earthworms — making them especially valuable in a worm bin. Note: if using tea bags, check that they're paper-based and not the plastic-mesh type, which don't decompose.

Used tea leaves Paper tea bags Coffee grounds Coffee filters (paper)
🥚

Eggshells & nutshells

Eggshells are a brilliant compost addition — they're rich in calcium carbonate, which helps balance the pH of compost and improves soil structure. They take longer to break down than soft scraps, so crushing them before adding to the bin speeds things up.

Most nutshells (walnut, almond, coconut) are also compostable, though harder shells like walnut can take 1–2 years. Coconut shells are better used as bowls, scrubbers, or plant pots before composting. Avoid adding salted or flavoured nutshells — the salt is harmful to soil microbes.

Eggshells Groundnut shells Almond shells Coconut shell Cashew shells
🌸

Floral & garden waste

Wilted flowers, fallen leaves, dried petals, small plant trimmings — all of this is wet waste. In fact, dry leaves are one of the most valuable additions to a home compost bin because they provide the carbon balance that food scraps (which are high in nitrogen) need.

If you have a garden or balcony, fallen leaves and small stem cuttings are an endless supply of free composting material. Avoid adding diseased plants to your compost — the pathogens can survive and spread to your garden later.

Wilted flowers Dried petals Fallen leaves Grass clippings Plant trimmings Pooja flowers
💆

Hair, nails & natural fibres

This one surprises most people — human hair, nail clippings, and natural fibre scraps (cotton, wool, jute, silk) are all biodegradable and compostable. Hair is particularly high in nitrogen and decomposes slowly, making it a good slow-release fertiliser in a compost pile.

Only natural fibres count — synthetic fibres like polyester, nylon, or acrylic are plastic and do not belong in wet waste.

Human & pet hair Nail clippings Cotton scraps Wool threads Jute fibres

What goes in — and what doesn't

Not everything organic belongs in the wet waste or compost bin. Some items attract pests, create odours, or simply don't break down well at home.

✓ Goes in

  • Raw fruit & vegetable scraps
  • Cooked food (small amounts)
  • Tea leaves & coffee grounds
  • Eggshells (crushed)
  • Fallen leaves & plant trimmings
  • Paper tea bags
  • Hair & nail clippings
  • Pooja flowers
  • Plain cooked rice & roti
  • Fruit peels & cores

✗ Keep out

  • Meat, fish & bones
  • Dairy products
  • Oily or greasy food
  • Plastic-mesh tea bags
  • Salted or flavoured waste
  • Diseased plants
  • Synthetic fibres
  • Treated or painted wood
  • Pet waste (in home compost)
  • Coal or charcoal ash

Common mistakes to avoid

Watch out for these

  • Mixing wet and dry waste. This is the most common mistake. A wet banana peel in your dry recyclables contaminates the whole bin — paper absorbs moisture, cardboard goes soggy, and none of it can be recycled anymore.
  • Using plastic bin liners for wet waste. Plastic bags defeat the purpose — the waste can't be composted with the bag around it. Use newspaper sheets to line the bin instead, or a compostable liner.
  • Not draining excess liquid. Very watery waste (leftover soup, coconut water) creates a soggy, smelly bin. Drain liquids before adding food scraps.
  • Adding meat or dairy to home compost. These attract rodents, create bad odours, and decompose in a way that home compost bins can't manage. They belong in a biogas unit or municipal wet waste collection — not your backyard pit.

What to do with wet waste

Once you've sorted your wet waste correctly, you have three good options for what to do with it — each suited to different living situations.

Option 1 — Compost it at home

The most self-sufficient option. A kitchen compost bin or backyard pit turns your scraps into rich manure in 30–45 days. Works even in apartments with a balcony. We cover this in detail in the next post.

Option 2 — Feed a home biogas unit

If you generate a lot of kitchen waste, a home biogas unit converts it directly into cooking gas and liquid fertiliser. Compact models now fit a small kitchen or backyard. We cover this too in an upcoming post.

Option 3 — Give it to municipal collection

In Kerala, the Haritha Karma Sena collects segregated wet waste from homes. Keep it in a green bin or covered container and hand it over on collection days — already sorted, not mixed with dry waste.

Sorting wet waste correctly is genuinely one of the highest-impact things you can do at home. It keeps organic material out of landfills, reduces methane emissions, and gives you something useful — compost for your plants or gas for your kitchen — in return.

Start simple: one green bin, lined with newspaper. Sort your kitchen scraps into it every day. That single habit, done consistently, makes a real difference.
