Composting at home —
a complete guide

August 11, 2026

Composting is the oldest form of recycling. Long before waste management was a concept, kitchen scraps went back into the earth and came back as food. Home composting is simply reviving that idea — turning what your kitchen throws away into something your garden needs.

The good news is that composting at home is far simpler than most people think. You don't need a garden, a lot of space, or any special skill. A balcony, a clay pot, and a little patience is all it takes to get started.

"Composting is nature's way of recycling. Every peel, every tea bag, every fallen leaf has a second life — if you let it."

How composting actually works

Composting is the natural process by which microorganisms — bacteria, fungi, and worms — break down organic material into a dark, crumbly, nutrient-rich substance called humus or compost. It's essentially controlled decomposition.

For composting to work well, it needs four things in balance:

The four ingredients

  • Greens (nitrogen-rich). Wet, fresh material — vegetable peels, fruit scraps, tea leaves, fresh grass clippings. These provide nitrogen, which feeds the microbes.
  • Browns (carbon-rich). Dry material — fallen leaves, cardboard, dry coconut husk, newspaper. These provide carbon and keep the pile airy and odour-free.
  • Moisture. The pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge — damp but not dripping. Too dry and decomposition slows; too wet and it gets smelly.
  • Air. Microbes need oxygen. Turning or aerating the pile regularly speeds up composting and prevents the anaerobic bacteria that cause bad odours.

The ideal ratio is roughly 2 parts browns to 1 part greens by volume. In practice, this means every time you add kitchen scraps, add a roughly equal handful of dry leaves or torn cardboard on top.

Three methods — pick what suits you

There's no single right way to compost. The best method depends on how much space you have, how much waste you generate, and how hands-on you want to be.

🌿

Pit composting

Garden / yard

The simplest and oldest method — dig a pit in the ground, add organic waste in layers, cover with soil, and let nature do the work. Ideal if you have a garden or yard, as the pit can be dug directly where you want to enrich the soil.

How to do it: Dig a pit about 2 feet deep and 2–3 feet wide. Add kitchen scraps and garden waste in layers, alternating wet scraps with dry leaves or soil. Cover loosely after each addition to deter pests. Turn the pile every 2–3 weeks with a spade. In 45–60 days, the bottom layer will be ready to use.

Time to compost45–60 days
Space neededSmall garden or yard
EffortLow — turn once a week
Best forHouses with outdoor space
🪴

Pot / bin composting

Apartment friendly

Perfect for apartments and homes without outdoor space. A clay pot, plastic drum, or purpose-built compost bin with drainage holes works well. You can keep it on a balcony, terrace, or even inside a kitchen with good ventilation.

How to do it: Start with a layer of dry leaves or cocopeat at the bottom. Add kitchen scraps, then cover with another layer of dry leaves or cocopeat. Sprinkle a little compost activator powder or a spoon of old compost to introduce microbes. Keep a loose lid on top. Add scraps daily and cover each time. Turn with a stick or hand fork every few days. In 30–45 days you'll have usable compost at the bottom.

Tip: Keep two bins and alternate — fill one for 2–3 weeks while the other rests and matures. This gives you a continuous supply of compost.

Time to compost30–45 days
Space neededBalcony or terrace
EffortLow — stir every few days
Best forApartments & small homes
🪱

Vermicomposting

Fastest & richest

Vermicomposting uses earthworms — specifically red wigglers — to break down organic waste faster than microbial composting alone. The result, called vermicompost or worm castings, is one of the richest organic fertilisers available — far more nutrient-dense than regular compost.

How to do it: Set up a worm bin — a shallow box or bin with drainage holes and a lid. Add bedding (torn cardboard, dry leaves, cocopeat) and introduce red wiggler worms. Feed them small amounts of kitchen scraps — vegetable peels, fruit scraps, coffee grounds — every 2–3 days. Keep the bin moist and in a cool, shaded spot. Avoid adding citrus, onion, garlic, meat, or dairy as these are harmful to worms. In 4–6 weeks you'll have dark, crumbly worm castings ready to use. The liquid that drains from the bottom (worm tea) is a powerful liquid fertiliser — dilute 1:10 with water before using.

Note: Worms are sensitive to heat — keep the bin below 35°C. In Kerala's climate, shade and ventilation are important.

Time to compost4–6 weeks
Space neededA small bin, indoors or balcony
EffortMedium — feed every 2–3 days
Best forAnyone who wants the richest compost

Starting your first bin — step by step

1

Choose your bin or spot

If you have a garden — pick a shaded corner and either dig a pit or place a bin there. If you're in an apartment — a clay pot with holes drilled at the bottom or a purpose-built compost tumbler on the balcony works well. Make sure there's some drainage so excess moisture can escape.

2

Start with a brown base

Before adding any kitchen scraps, lay a base layer of dry material — fallen leaves, torn cardboard, cocopeat, or dry coconut husk — about 3–4 inches deep. This creates drainage, introduces carbon, and gives the pile a good foundation.

3

Add scraps and cover

Add your kitchen scraps — peels, coffee grounds, eggshells — and immediately cover with a layer of dry leaves or cocopeat. This is the most important habit: always cover greens with browns. It prevents odours, deters fruit flies, and keeps the carbon-nitrogen balance right.

4

Keep it moist — not wet

The pile should feel like a damp sponge. In Kerala's humid climate, the pile often stays moist enough from the scraps alone. If it looks dry, sprinkle a little water. If it's too wet or smelly, add more dry leaves or torn cardboard and mix it in.

5

Turn it regularly

Use a stick, hand fork, or compost aerator to turn and mix the pile every 3–5 days. This introduces oxygen, speeds up decomposition, and prevents the pile from compacting. If you're short on time, even once a week makes a significant difference over not turning at all.

6

Harvest your compost

When the bottom of the pile looks dark, crumbly, earthy-smelling, and you can no longer identify the original materials — it's ready. This usually takes 30–60 days depending on the method and climate. Scoop it out from the bottom, sieve if needed, and use it in your garden or potted plants. Mix 1 part compost with 3 parts soil for best results.

Troubleshooting — common problems

Smells bad

Too much green material or too wet. Add dry leaves or torn cardboard and mix well. Make sure you're covering scraps after every addition. Avoid adding meat, dairy, or oily food to home compost.

Fruit flies

Scraps are exposed. Always bury or cover food scraps under a layer of dry leaves or cocopeat. A tight-fitting lid on your bin also helps. You can also add a thin layer of soil on top.

Not breaking down

Too dry, too much brown material, or too cold. Add a little water and some fresh green scraps. Sprinkle compost accelerator powder to reintroduce microbes. Turn the pile to aerate it.

Pile attracts ants or pests

Usually because sugary fruit scraps are exposed at the top. Bury them deeper and cover well. Sprinkling a little neem powder on top can also deter pests without harming the composting process.
