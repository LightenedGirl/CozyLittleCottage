Biogas

Setting up a biogas system —
at home

August 12, 2026

Links marked amazon.in and amazon.com are affiliate links.

Imagine feeding your kitchen waste into a small unit in your backyard every morning — and using the gas it produces to cook dinner that evening. That's exactly what a home biogas system does, and it's more accessible than most people realise.

Home biogas has been quietly growing in India, particularly in Kerala, where the climate is ideal for it. If you generate a good amount of wet waste — food scraps, cooked leftovers, vegetable peels — a biogas unit can turn that into cooking fuel and liquid fertiliser simultaneously.

"Your kitchen waste has energy in it. A biogas system is simply a way of collecting it before it escapes into the atmosphere."

What is biogas — and how does it work?

Biogas is a mixture of gases — primarily methane (50–70%) and carbon dioxide — produced when organic matter breaks down in the absence of oxygen. This process is called anaerobic digestion.

In a biogas unit, wet organic waste (food scraps, kitchen waste, sometimes animal dung) is fed into a sealed digester tank along with water. Inside, anaerobic bacteria break the material down over several days, producing biogas that collects in a storage dome or bag above the digester. The gas is then piped directly to your kitchen stove for cooking.

The leftover slurry — called digestate or bioslurry — drains out of the unit and is one of the richest liquid fertilisers available. Diluted with water, it feeds plants dramatically faster than compost alone.

Biogas vs composting — key difference

Both handle wet waste. Composting is aerobic (uses oxygen) and produces solid compost. Biogas digestion is anaerobic (no oxygen) and produces gas for cooking plus liquid fertiliser. Biogas handles meat, fish, and dairy that composting can't — making it more suitable for households that generate those scraps.

Kerala advantage

Kerala's warm, humid climate is nearly perfect for biogas production. The Kerala government has also subsidised home biogas units through various schemes — check with your local panchayat or KSEB office for current eligibility. Units like the Sintex biogas plant and Home Biogas systems have been widely adopted across the state.

Types of home biogas units

Fixed dome digester

The traditional design — a dome-shaped underground concrete tank where waste is fed in, gas collects at the top, and slurry exits from the side. Very durable and long-lasting (20–30 years), but requires construction and can't be relocated.

This is the type most commonly subsidised by state governments and is widely used in Kerala. Best for permanent homes with a garden or backyard.

Portable / compact biogas unit

These are the easiest entry point for most urban households and don't require any government approval or professional installation beyond basic plumbing.

Floating drum digester

A two-tank design where an inverted steel drum floats on the slurry and rises as gas fills it, providing a visual indicator of gas pressure. More common in rural areas and farms. Reliable, but the steel drum requires occasional maintenance to prevent rusting.

Less common in urban homes now that portable plastic units are available, but still a good option if you're building a permanent setup on a farm or large property.

Capacity2–10 cubic metres
Cost₹20,000–₹50,000
Lifespan15–20 years (drum needs upkeep)
InstallationProfessional required

Setting up a portable unit — step by step

For most urban and semi-urban homes, a compact portable biogas unit is the most practical starting point. Here's how to set one up.

1

Choose your location

Pick a spot that gets some sunlight (warmth speeds up digestion) and is close enough to the kitchen for the gas pipe to reach. A backyard, side yard, or large terrace all work. The unit needs a flat, stable surface. Keep it away from the main sitting areas as there can be a mild odour near the inlet when feeding.

2

Assemble the unit

Most portable units come with an instruction manual and all fittings. Connect the digester tank, gas storage bag or dome, inlet pipe (where you feed waste), outlet pipe (where slurry drains), and gas outlet pipe that runs to the kitchen. Most setups take 2–4 hours and require only basic plumbing tools — no special skills needed.

3

Prime the digester

Before regular use, you need to introduce anaerobic bacteria to start the digestion process. Fill the digester with a mix of water and either: cattle dung slurry (the most effective starter), previously made biogas slurry from another unit, or a commercial biogas starter inoculant. Let it sit for 7–10 days before feeding kitchen waste. During this time, gas will begin to form — but let the first 1–2 batches of gas vent out as they contain mostly CO₂ and won't burn well.

4

Start feeding daily

Once primed, feed kitchen waste daily — ideally blended or chopped finely with water into a slurry before adding. A ratio of roughly 1 part waste to 1 part water works well. You can feed vegetable scraps, cooked food, fruit peels, tea waste, and even small amounts of meat and dairy — unlike composting, biogas digesters handle them all. Avoid feeding citrus in large quantities, very oily waste, or any synthetic material.

5

Connect to your stove

Run the gas pipe from the unit to your kitchen and connect to a biogas-compatible burner. Most portable units include a burner, or you can use a standard LPG burner with minor adjustment. The gas pressure from a home unit is lower than LPG, so the flame will be smaller — sufficient for boiling, cooking rice, making tea, and general cooking. For high-heat cooking like deep frying, LPG may still be needed as backup.

6

Collect and use the slurry

As you feed the inlet daily, processed slurry exits from the outlet pipe. Collect it in a bucket or channel it directly to a garden bed. This bioslurry is an exceptional liquid fertiliser — rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, and immediately available to plants (unlike compost which needs to break down further in soil). Dilute 1 part slurry with 5–10 parts water before applying to plants to prevent burning roots.

What to feed — and what to avoid

Feed freely

  • All vegetable and fruit scraps — peels, cores, trimmings, overripe fruit
  • Cooked food — rice, roti, dal, curry, bread, noodles
  • Meat and fish scraps — including bones (softer ones break down; hard bones can clog)
  • Dairy waste — spoiled milk, curd, cheese (in moderate amounts)
  • Tea and coffee waste — leaves, grounds, paper filters
  • Eggshells — crushed if possible

Avoid or limit

  • Large amounts of citrus — the acidity disrupts the bacteria. Small amounts are fine.
  • Heavily oily or fatty waste — slows digestion and can coat the bacteria.
  • Antibiotics or medicines — kill the anaerobic bacteria the unit depends on.
  • Disinfectants or cleaning chemicals — even small amounts can sterilise the digester.
  • Non-organic material — plastic, glass, metal, synthetic fibre — anything that won't decompose.

Ongoing maintenance

Home biogas units are low maintenance once running. Here's what to keep on top of:

Monthly checks

  • Check gas pipe connections for leaks — apply soapy water to joints and look for bubbles.
  • Clear the inlet and outlet pipes if slurry flow slows — a clog usually means the waste needs more water when feeding.
  • Check the gas storage bag for damage if your unit uses one — UV exposure degrades plastic over time.
  • Monitor gas output. If it drops significantly, the bacteria may need a boost — add a small amount of fresh cattle dung slurry or biogas starter.
