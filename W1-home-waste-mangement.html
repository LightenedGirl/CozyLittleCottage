Home Waste Management —
a simple guide

July 20, 2026

This post contains general guidance on household waste management. Disposal rules may vary by local municipality.

Every household generates waste every single day — kitchen scraps, cardboard, old batteries, broken gadgets. Most of it ends up in one bin and heads straight to a landfill. But with a little sorting and a few good habits, the majority of what we throw away can have a second life.

The idea isn't to be perfect. It's to be a little more intentional about where things go.

"The best waste is the waste you never create. Everything else is just good sorting."

The five categories of home waste

Most household waste falls into one of five types. Once you know what goes where, the whole thing becomes second nature.

1
Start here

Reduce — before waste even exists

The most effective waste strategy is making less of it. Carry your own bags, bottles, and containers. Say no to single-use plastic. Choose products with minimal packaging, and buy only what you'll actually use.

It sounds small, but this step quietly handles a large chunk of the problem before it starts.

→ Think before you buy. Do you really need it?
2
Green bin

Wet waste — compostable and organic

Vegetable peels, fruit scraps, cooked food leftovers, tea bags, coffee grounds, eggshells — anything that was once alive and will rot. This is nearly 60% of what most kitchens throw away.

All of it can go into a compost bin or pit instead of a landfill. Even a small clay pot on a balcony works. In 30–45 days, you have rich soil for your plants.

→ Line your bin with newspaper instead of a plastic bag.
3
Blue bin

Dry waste — recyclable materials

Paper, cardboard, plastic bottles, glass jars, aluminium cans, metal scraps — these can all be recycled and remade into something new. The key rule: keep them clean and dry.

A greasy pizza box or a wet plastic bottle can contaminate an entire batch at the recycling plant. Rinse, flatten, and store separately from wet waste.

→ Clean before recycling. Contamination wastes the effort.
4
Red bin · handle with care

Hazardous waste — never in the regular bin

Batteries, CFL bulbs, expired medicines, paints, solvents, pesticides, and cleaning chemicals all contain substances that can leach into soil and groundwater if thrown in a regular bin.

Keep a small labelled box in a safe corner at home and drop it off at a designated collection point when it's full. Many pharmacies take back expired medicines, and municipalities often have periodic collection drives.

→ Store safely. Drop off — don't bin.
5
Authorised recycler

E-waste — electronic scrap

Old phones, laptops, chargers, cables, remote controls, and broken appliances. These contain both valuable materials — gold, copper, lithium — and toxic ones like lead and mercury. They should never go into a regular bin.

Most phone and appliance brands have authorised take-back programs. You can also find certified e-waste recyclers in your city who will collect from your doorstep.

→ Check your brand's take-back program before discarding old devices.

A few habits that actually help

Quick tips

  • Segregate at source. Sort waste right where it's made — the kitchen, bathroom, desk. Mixing everything and sorting later is far less effective.
  • Donate before discarding. Clothes, books, furniture, and working electronics in reasonable condition can find a new home instead of a landfill.
  • Compost your kitchen scraps. It takes very little space and turns your food waste into something genuinely useful for your garden or balcony plants.
  • Rinse recyclables. A quick rinse before storing dry waste prevents odours and keeps your batch clean for the recycler.
  • Skip the plastic bin liner. Line your wet waste bin with a sheet of newspaper instead — it absorbs moisture and composts along with the rest.

If you're in Kerala

The Haritha Karma Sena — local waste workers — collect segregated waste from most homes on a schedule. Keeping wet and dry waste separated before they arrive makes a real difference to how well the material gets processed downstream.

None of this requires a complete lifestyle overhaul. Start with one bin for wet waste, one for dry. That single habit, done consistently, already puts you well ahead of where most households are.

Small, steady choices add up — in your home, your neighbourhood, and eventually in the soil and water around us.
